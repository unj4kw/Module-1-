Project Title:¶

Tracking Cognitive Decline in Alzheimer's Disease: Tau vs. Amyloid and the Role of Education

Project Goal:¶

This project seeks to identify all factors associated with cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease by analyzing data from 84 brain donors, half of them having dementia. First, we will use scatterplots and correlation analysis to determine which protein; amyloid-beta or tau, is closely correlated with cognitive impairment as measured by MMSE scores. Second, among donors with high Alzheimer's pathology, is higher education associated with better MMSE scores or lower rates of dementia? Does higher education protect against dementia even when the brain shows Alzheimer's damage? We define “high pathology” as donors with an Overall AD Neuropathological Change rating of “High”, meaning they showed advanced amyloid plaque spread (Thal phase 4-5), advanced tau tangle spread (Braak stage V-VI), and moderate to frequent plaque density (CERAD). The answers could point towards targets for treatment and toward lifestyle factors that may help prevent or delay dementia. Although, this observational analysis cannot establish causation.

Disease Background:¶

Prevalence & incidence: Prevalence is the proportion of a population living with a disease at a given time, while incidence is the rate of new cases per year. In 2025, an estimated 7.2 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's dementia, about 1 in 9 people in that age group (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Prevalence rises sharply with age, from 5.1% of people age 65–74 to 33.4% of people age 85 and older, and annual incidence similarly increases from 0.4% at ages 65–74 to 7.6% at age 85+ (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Nearly two-thirds of Americans with Alzheimer's are women (4.4 million vs. 2.8 million men), and Black (19%) and Hispanic (14%) older adults have higher dementia prevalence than White older adults (10%) (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Although the age-specific rate of new cases has declined in some studies since the 1970s, the total number of people with Alzheimer's is growing because the population is aging; by 2030, all baby boomers will be 65 or older (Alzheimer's Association, 2025).

Economic burden: Health and long-term care costs for people with Alzheimer's and other dementias are projected to reach $384 billion in 2025 and nearly $1 trillion by 2050 (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). In addition, family members and other unpaid caregivers provided care valued at about $413.4 billion in 2024 (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Much of this burden falls on families through out-of-pocket costs, lost income, and caregiver strain.

Risk factors (genetic, lifestyle): Increasing age is the greatest risk factor for Alzheimer's dementia (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). The APOE-e4 allele is the best-known common genetic risk factor, and its association with Alzheimer's may be stronger in women than in men (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). The lifetime risk of Alzheimer's at age 45 is about 1 in 5 for women and 1 in 10 for men (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Modifiable risk factors include hypertension, smoking, midlife obesity, and lower educational attainment (Alzheimer's Association, 2025).

Societal determinants: Social and environmental conditions strongly shape dementia risk. Lower education, lower socioeconomic status, and limited access to quality health care are associated with higher risk and delayed diagnosis, and a lack of culturally competent providers can widen disparities (DC Department of Health, n.d.; Walsh et al., n.d.). Environmental factors such as air pollution, unsafe housing, and inadequate transportation also negatively affect brain health (DC Department of Health, n.d.). Sexual and gender minority older adults may face increased risk because of discrimination, minority stress, and exclusion from social support (Alzheimer's Association, 2025; Walsh et al., n.d.).

Symptoms: Alzheimer's first damages neurons in brain regions responsible for memory, language, and thinking, so early symptoms typically include difficulty remembering recent conversations, names, or events (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). As the disease progresses, people may develop confusion about time or place, poor judgment, difficulty completing familiar tasks, and changes in mood, personality, or behavior (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). In late stages, individuals may have difficulty walking, speaking, and swallowing.

Diagnosis: Diagnosis combines a medical history, physical and neurological exam, and cognitive tests such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the same test used in our dataset (Mayo Clinic, n.d.). Brain imaging supports diagnosis: MRI can show shrinkage of affected brain regions, FDG-PET shows areas of reduced glucose metabolism, and amyloid and tau PET scans detect plaques and tangles directly (Mayo Clinic, n.d.; Bomasang-Layno & Bronsther, 2021). Newer blood tests, such as PrecivityAD, measure amyloid-beta in the blood to help identify Alzheimer's pathology less invasively (Bomasang-Layno & Bronsther, 2021).

Standard of care treatments (& reimbursement): Lecanemab (Leqembi®) and donanemab (Kisunla™) are FDA-approved antibodies that remove amyloid-beta from the brain and modestly slow disease progression in people with early Alzheimer's (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Symptomatic medications, including cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine, and drugs such as brexpiprazole for agitation help manage symptoms but do not slow the disease (Mayo Clinic, n.d.; Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Non-drug approaches such as physical activity, music and art therapy, and reminiscence therapy support quality of life (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Medicare covers anti-amyloid antibodies for eligible patients, but the cost of the drugs, required PET scans, and monitoring MRIs can limit access.

Disease progression & prognosis: Alzheimer's is progressive: people age 65 and older survive an average of four to eight years after diagnosis, though some live as long as 20 years (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Progression is commonly described in seven stages, where stages 1–3 occur before dementia (no impairment, subjective decline, and mild cognitive impairment) and stages 4–7 describe mild, moderate, severe, and very severe dementia. Alzheimer's kills more people than breast cancer and prostate cancer combined (Alzheimer's Association, 2025).

Continuum of care providers: Primary care physicians and advanced practice providers are usually the first point of contact and coordinate care (National Institute on Aging, n.d.-a). Specialists include neurologists, geriatricians, geriatric psychiatrists, and neuropsychologists, often working through memory clinics. As the disease advances, care shifts to home health aides, adult day programs, assisted living and nursing homes, and eventually hospice, with nearly 12 million unpaid family caregivers providing much of the daily support (Alzheimer's Association, 2025).

Biological mechanisms (anatomy, organ physiology, cell & molecular physiology): Alzheimer's is defined by two hallmark changes: clumps of the protein fragment amyloid-beta that form plaques outside neurons, and an abnormal form of the protein tau that forms tangles inside neurons (National Institute on Aging, n.d.-b). These changes are accompanied by inflammation, reduced glucose use by the brain, loss of synapses, and neuronal death, leading to brain atrophy (National Institute on Aging, n.d.-b). Both proteins spread through the brain in predictable stages, measured by the Thal phase (amyloid) and Braak stage (tau) ratings in our dataset. Autopsy studies show that cognitive impairment correlates more closely with tau tangles than with amyloid plaques (Nelson et al., 2012; Bennett et al., 2004), which motivated our first research question.

Clinical trials/next-gen therapies: Clinical trials showed that lecanemab and donanemab slow cognitive and functional decline in people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia with confirmed amyloid, though neither is a cure (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Next-generation therapies aim to improve delivery and start treatment earlier. Trontinemab is an experimental anti-amyloid antibody attached to a "Brainshuttle" that uses the transferrin receptor to cross the blood-brain barrier more efficiently, and Roche's Phase 3 Preventron trial is testing it in cognitively unimpaired people at high risk of Alzheimer's (Meglio, n.d.). Because tau tracks cognition more closely than amyloid, therapies targeting tau are also an important area of research.

def paired_values(pts, x_attr, y_attr):
    # Builds two matching lists (x and y) for a scatter plot.
    # Only keeps patients who have both values, so a missing value doesn't break the list.
    # getattr(p, x_attr) gets an attribute using its name as a string, e.g. getattr(p,'ptau') is the same as p.ptau.
    keep = [p for p in pts if getattr(p, x_attr) is not None and getattr(p, y_attr) is not None]
    return[getattr(p, x_attr) for p in keep], [getattr(p, y_attr) for p in keep]

def iqr_outlier_bounds(values):
    # Finds the cutoffs for outliers using the IQR rule.
    # quantiles(n = 4) splits the data into 4 equal parts and returns Q1 (25%), the median, and Q3 (75%).
    # The _ skips the median since we don't need it here.
    q1, _, q3 = statistics.quantiles(values, n = 4)
    iqr = q3 - q1 # IQR = the spread of the middle 50% of the data.
    # Anything below Q1 - 1.5*IQR or above Q3 + 1.5*IQR counts as an outlier.
    return q1 - 1.5 * iqr, q3 + 1.5 * iqr

def linear_regression(x, y):
    # Fits a straight line (y = slope * x + intercept) through the data, following the method from lecture.
    X = np.array(x).reshape(-1,1) # sklearn needs a single column, so reshape(-1, 1) turns the list into one.
    Y = np.array(y)
    model = LinearRegression() # Creates an empty linear regression model.
    model.fit(X, Y) # Finds the line that best fits the data.
    slope = model.coef_[0] # How much y changes for every 1 unit increase in x.
    intercept = model.intercept_ #
    r2 = model.score(X, Y) # R^2 represents how much of the variation in y is explained by x (0 = poor fit, 1 = perfect fit).
    return model, slope, intercept, r2

def scatter_with_regression(ax, x, y, xlabel, ylabel, title):
    # Draws a scatter plot with its regression line, and writes the equation and R^2 value on the graph.
    model, slope, intercept, r2 = linear_regression(x, y)
    X = np.array(x).reshape(-1, 1)

    # ax.scatter plots a dot per patient.
    # alpha = 0.7 makes the dots slightly see-through to help show overlapping dots.
    ax.scatter(x, y, color = 'blue', alpha = 0.7, edgecolor = 'black')

    # model.predict(X) calculates the y-value on the line for each x, and ax.plot draws the red regression line.
    ax.plot(X, model.predict(X), color = 'red')

    # Writes the equation and R^2 in a white box in the bottom-left corner.
    # transform = ax.transAxes means (0.03, 0.03) is a position on the graph (3% from the left and bottom), not a data value.
    ax.text(0.03, 0.03, f'y = {slope:.3f}x + {intercept:.2f}\nR² = {r2:.3f}', transform = ax.transAxes, color = 'red', fontsize = 9, va = 'bottom', bbox = dict(facecolor = 'white', alpha = 0.8))
    ax.set_xlabel(xlabel) # x-axis label
    ax.set_ylabel(ylabel) # y-axis label
    ax.set_title(title) # graph title
    return slope, intercept, r2

def significance_stars(p):
    # Turns a p-value into the standard star shorthand shown above the bars.
    # We use alpha = 0.05, so any p-value below 0.05 is significant. More stars indicates stronger evidence.
    if p < 0.001: return '***'
    if p < 0.01: return '**'
    if p < 0.05: return '*'
    return 'not significant'

def standardize(values):
    # Converts values into z-scores: (value - mean) / standard deviation.
    # This lets the K-S test compare the data to a standard normal curve (mean 0, stdev 1).
    m = statistics.mean(values)
    sd = statistics.stdev(values)
    return[(v - m) / sd for v in values]

def check_assumptions(*groups):
# Before comparing groups, we run two checks to decide which kind of test is appropriate.
# *groups means this function can take any number of groups (2 for a t-test, 4 for the ANOVA).
    
    # Check 1: Normality (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test):
    # Each group is converted to z-scores and compared to a standard normal curve.
    # If p > 0.05, the data is not significantly different from normal, so we treat it as normal.
    normal = all(stats.kstest(standardize(g), 'norm').pvalue > 0.05 for g in groups)     # all(...) only makes it True if every group passes.

    # CHECK 2 - Equal variances (Levene's test):
    # If p > 0.05, the spreads are not significantly different, so we treat the variances as equal.
    equal_var = stats.levene(*groups).pvalue > 0.05

    # Prints the result of both checks so we can report which test was chosen and why.
    print(f"Assumption checks: normal (K-S) = {normal}, equal variances (Levene) = {equal_var}")
    
    # Returns True only if both checks pass.
    # True  -> use a parametric test, which compares means (Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA).
    # False -> use a non-parametric test, which compares ranks (Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis).
    return normal and equal_var

def two_group_test(a, b):
# Compares exactly two unpaired groups. There are different patients in each group and each one is measured once.
# This is two-tailed because we are testing whether the groups differ, not whether one is specifically higher.
# If both assumptions pass, it proceeds to the student's t-test, to compare means.
    if check_assumptions(a, b):
        t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_ind(a, b)
        return "Student's t-test", t_stat, p_val
        # If either assumption fails, it proceeds to the Mann-Whitney rank sum test, to compare ranks instead of means.
    u_stat, p_val = stats.mannwhitneyu(a, b, alternative = 'two-sided')
    return "Mann-Whitney", u_stat, p_val

def bar_with_test(ax, groups, labels, ylabel, title, colors):
# Draws a bar graph of the mean (+/- standard deviation) for two groups and adds the test result on top.
    means = [statistics.mean(g) for g in groups] # bar heights
    stds = [statistics.stdev(g) for g in groups] # error bar sizes (+/- stdev)

    # ax.bar draws the bars 
    # yerr adds the error bars
    # capsize adds caps to their ends
    # edgecolor outlines each bar
    ax.bar(labels, means, yerr = stds, capsize = 8, color = colors, edgecolor = 'black')
    
    # Runs the statistical test that fits the data (t-test or Mann-Whitney).
    test_name, stat, p = two_group_test(groups[0], groups[1])
    # Finds the top of the tallest error bar so the bracket and p-value can go right above it.
    top = max(m + s for m, s in zip(means, stds))
    
    # Draws the bracket connecting the two bars. The x-values are the bar positions and the y-values set its height.
    ax.plot([0, 0, 1, 1], [top * 1.05, top * 1.1, top * 1.1, top * 1.05], color = 'black')

    # Writes the stars, the test name, and the p-value centered above the bracket.
    ax.text(0.5, top * 1.12, f'{significance_stars(p)}\n{test_name}, p = {p:.3g}', ha = 'center', fontsize = 8)
    
    # Makes the y-axis tall enough so the bracket and text aren't cut off.
    ax.set_ylim(0, top * 1.35)
    ax.set_ylabel(ylabel)
    ax.set_title(title)

    # Places the tick marks under each bar and labels them with the group name and its number of patients (n).
    ax.set_xticks(range(len(labels)))
    ax.set_xticklabels([f'{l}\n(n = {len(g)})' for l, g in zip(labels, groups)])
    return test_name, stat, p

# Figure 3: One-way ANOVA to visualize if pTAU differs across the 4 levels of AD pathology.
# Null hypothesis: pTAU is the same across all 4 pathology levels.

# The 4 pathology levels, from least to most damage.
levels = ['Not AD', 'Low', 'Intermediate', 'High']

# Builds one list of pTAU values per pathology level using the ad_change filter.
# This creates a list of 4 lists, one for each level.
ptau_groups = [[p.ptau for p in Patient.filter_patients(patients, ad_change = lvl) if p.ptau is not None] for lvl in levels]

# Checks the assumptions first. The * unpacks the 4 lists so each one is passed in as its own group.
if check_assumptions(*ptau_groups):
    # If both checks pass, it runs a one-way ANOVA to compare means.
    anova_name = 'One-way ANOVA'
    stat, anova_p = stats.f_oneway(*ptau_groups)
else:
    # If either check fails, it runs a Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA on ranks.
    anova_name = 'Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA on ranks'
    stat, anova_p = stats.kruskal(*ptau_groups)
print(f"{anova_name}: statistic = {stat:.3f}, p = {anova_p:.3g}\n")

# ANOVA only tells us that at least one group is different, not which one.
# So if it passes (p <= 0.05), Tukey's HSD post hoc test compares every pair of groups to find which ones differ.
if anova_p <= 0.05:
    tukey = stats.tukey_hsd(*ptau_groups)
    print("Tukey HSD post hoc test (pairs with p < 0.05 differ significantly): ")

    # The two loops go through every pair of levels once
    # tukey.pvalue[i, j] is the p-value for the pair of level i and level j

Verify and validate your analysis:¶

We ran several checks to make sure our results were not caused by data errors, incorrect statistical tests, or extreme values. First, we checked for impossible values. One donor had an MMSE score of 33, even though the maximum score is 30, so we removed this value from all MMSE analyses. Next, before each group comparison, we checked whether the data met the requirements for parametric tests. We used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for normality and Levene’s test for equal variances. When the data did not meet these requirements, we used a non-parametric test instead. Due to the protein data being highly skewed, we also checked for outliers using the IQR rule and box plots. We then repeated the Figure 1 regressions without the outliers. This showed that the original ABeta40 relationship with MMSE (R2 = 0.333) was mostly caused by outliers. After removing them, the R2 dropped to 0.086. Without outliers, all four proteins had similarly weak relationships with MMSE (R2 ≈ 0.06-0.09). We also repeated the pTAU and ABeta42 analyses using only donors whose MMSE was measured within 24 months of death (n = 36). pTAU became slightly stronger (R2 = 0.108), while ABeta42 became weaker (R2 = 0.043). Overall, pTAU was the only protein whose negative relationship with MMSE remained consistent across all of these checks.

We compared our results with previous studies. Our Question 1 results generally matched the literature. Previous research found that cognitive decline is more strongly related to tau pathology than amyloid pathology (Nelson et al., 2012). Bennett et al. (2004) also found that amyloid was no longer significantly associated with Alzheimer’s disease after accounting for tau tangles. This supports our finding that pTAU was the most consistent predictor of MMSE. Our ABeta42 result also matched previous research because ABeta42 was significantly higher in donors with dementia (p = 0.002), similar to findings from Bennett et al. (2004).

However, our Question 2 results did not match the literature. We found no relationship between education and MMSE in the high-pathology group (R2 = 0.000) and no significant difference in education between the dementia and non-dementia groups (p = 0.219). Donors with dementia actually averaged slightly more education (16.4 vs. 15.2 years), the opposite of what cognitive reserve predicts. Bennett et al. (2005), however, found that education affects how strongly brain pathology impacts cognition. This supports the idea of cognitive reserve. The weakness of all our correlations is consistent with Nelson et al. (2012), who note that non-Alzheimer’s brain lesions common in older adults can weaken the link between pathology and cognition.

Several factors may have affected our results. Our high pathology group only had 11 donors without dementia, which made it harder to detect an education effect. Our sample was also highly educated overall, so there was not much variation in education levels. The MMSE is also a short screening test with a maximum score of 30, so it may not capture differences in cognition as well as more detailed cognitive tests. In addition, there was sometimes a large gap between when the MMSE was measured and when the brain proteins were measured after death. Our regressions also reported R2 but not p-values, so we could not formally test whether each regression slope was significantly different from zero. Finally, we did not account for other brain conditions in the dataset, such as LATE, Lewy bodies, and vascular disease, which can also affect cognition.

Conclusions and Ethical Implications:¶

Our results suggest that pTAU was more consistently linked to cognitive decline than amyloid-beta. pTAU was the only protein whose negative relationship with MMSE stayed consistent after removing outliers and using only more recent MMSE scores. It also increased in donors with the highest levels of pathology. However, all of the relationships were still weak (R² < 0.11), so these results alone do not show that pTAU is a strong predictor of cognitive decline. They do suggest that pTAU and tau-related biomarkers could be worth exploring alongside current amyloid-focused treatments.

For education, we did not find evidence that higher education protected high-pathology donors from dementia. However, this result may have been affected by our small and highly educated sample. A lack of a significant relationship does not mean that education or mental engagement are unimportant, especially since larger studies have found evidence for cognitive reserve. Finally, our study included 84 brain donors from one region, so the results may not apply to other or more diverse populations. Future research on tau-based diagnostics and treatments should include more diverse groups and consider making these treatments accessible regardless of income or insurance.

Limitations and Future Work:¶

Our analysis had several limitations. The sample was small (84 donors), and only 11 high-pathology donors did not have dementia, which made it harder to detect an education effect. The sample was also highly educated, so there was not much variation in education levels. The MMSE is a short screening test, and some scores were taken years before death, while the proteins were measured after death from only one brain region, the middle temporal gyrus. There were also some statistical limitations. Our regressions reported R² without p-values, and we used Tukey’s HSD as our post hoc test even though it assumes equal variances, which our pTAU groups did not have. We did not control for age or other brain conditions, such as LATE, Lewy bodies, and vascular disease. Finally, because this was an observational study, our results show associations, not cause and effect. Future work should use larger and more diverse groups, control for age and other brain conditions, test whether education changes the relationship between pathology and cognition, and use repeated cognitive testing over time to better understand how tau and amyloid relate to cognitive decline.
